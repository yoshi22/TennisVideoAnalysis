# Offline iOS Tennis Rally Interval Detection

## Executive Summary

The highest-ROI path is **not** better ball tracking; it is a **direct rally-state model** that learns *play vs non-play* from the labels you already have, using court-cropped video, static-scene cues, player motion, and optional audio as auxiliary input. Related work shows compact end-to-end temporal models can spot tennis events very accurately, weak supervision can approach full-supervision quality, and practical sports systems can segment play without first solving perfect ball tracking.

If you eventually need richer tennis analytics, the best balls-first fallback is a **light, domain-adapted tracker with court priors and pseudo-labeling**, not a heavyweight broadcast-trained stack. Multi-sport training and explicit background modeling materially improve racket-sport ball tracking, but the newest robust trackers still add training and deployment complexity that is hard to justify when the end goal is only rally intervals.

Given your constraints, the most realistic plan to reach **Event F1 ≥ 0.85** is: **expand interval-labeled data first**, train a compact direct temporal segmentation model second, and postpone frame-level ball labels unless the direct approach plateaus or your roadmap requires bounce/trajectory analytics.

## Recommended Path

The best first implementation is a **direct rally-state model** that predicts **play / non-play** over time and then converts the prediction stream into rally intervals. That is the cleanest fit to your objective, and it avoids wasting effort on a harder intermediate problem that you do not actually need to solve yet. E2E-Spot is the strongest research signal here: it is compact, trains on a single GPU, and gets **96.1 mAP at 1-frame tolerance** on tennis precise spotting with a default configuration; the paper explicitly argues that end-to-end spatial-temporal learning beats two-phase pipelines that depend on generic pre-extracted features. In parallel, TAS work with sparse supervision shows you do **not** need dense frame-by-frame manual labels to get near-full-supervision performance.

The important strategic point is this: **you already own the right label type** for this route. Rally intervals can be rasterized into dense binary supervision at a downsampled frame rate with no new ontology. That is far more valuable than chasing perfect ball centers while still having only eight labeled clips. The weakest assumption in many failed sports-CV projects is that better low-level ball localization automatically yields better product-level event segmentation. That assumption is false surprisingly often, and your own TrackNet V1 result is already evidence of it. The product goal is rally boundary quality, so train directly on rally boundaries.

A practical first version should have four pieces. First, detect or estimate the **court region / homography** once per clip and ignore everything outside the playable court, because commercial practice and patents both use court topology as a first-class cue rather than treating the frame as generic video. Second, add a **static-scene representation** such as a background median image or background-difference channel; RacketVision’s strongest recent result is that explicit background modeling greatly reduces localization error in racket sports, including tennis. Third, feed court-cropped RGB plus player motion summaries, and optionally a lightweight audio onset envelope, into a **compact temporal model**. Fourth, enforce simple priors in post-processing: minimum rally length, hysteresis on transitions, and a short smoothing window. Those priors are cheap and align well with tennis structure.

For the backbone, the safest reference design is the **E2E-Spot default scale**. Its default model uses a small RegNet-Y 200MF + GRU stack, with roughly **4.5M total parameters** from the paper’s table and end-to-end training on 100-frame clips. That is in the right size class for offline iOS batching. With standard post-training quantization, both Core ML and TFLite support reducing precision to 8 bits or below-8-bit weight formats, which makes this path much more deployable than custom multi-frame tracking architectures.

On cost, this path is the cheapest credible option. The public E2E-Spot setup was built to train on a single GPU, and current rented-GPU pricing starts around **$0.50/hr** on Lambda’s cloud, with **A100 at $1.19/hr** on Runpod and **$3.09/hr** on Paperspace. A first serious prototype with a small number of hyperparameter sweeps is therefore a **tens-of-dollars problem**, not a large infra problem, and much of the early work can be done on Apple Silicon before renting any GPU at all.

The main risk is overfitting, not inference or export. Eight clips are simply too few to declare the direct path capped. Published successful sports models are usually trained on substantially more data: E2E-Spot’s tennis benchmark comes from **28 matches / 3,345 point clips / 33,791 frame-accurate events**; RacketVision’s tennis subset has **431 games, 150,399 frames, and 21,544 tennis ball annotations**. Your current dataset is below the normal data regime by an order of magnitude. If you judge the direct path only on eight clips, you are mostly measuring data starvation.

## Conditional Second Path

If you know the roadmap will require **ball-dependent features later**—shot speed, bounce maps, shot-type attribution, or line-call-like functionality—then the second-best path is **not** “re-try TrackNet V1 harder.” It is a **small, domain-adapted tracker with explicit court and static-scene priors**, plus pseudo-labeling. The most relevant new evidence is not from TrackNet V1; it is from the combination of TrackNetV4, RacketVision, and semi-supervised sports detection. TrackNetV4 shows modest but real gains over its baseline on a new tennis set (**86.53 → 88.05 / 88.13 F1**). RacketVision then adds the more important practical lesson: **multi-sport training** and **background modeling** matter a lot in racket sports, raising tennis mAP to **81.9** and reducing tennis MDE by **61.4%** when background median input is added.

That matters for your exact domain because your videos are **tripod-fixed** and amateur. A static camera is not just a nuisance; it is exploitable structure. A background median or background-subtracted channel is unusually valuable in this setting, because it tells the model what is stable and what is new. Your current motion-only baseline already discovered the downside of doing this with hand-crafted thresholds. The correct next step is not to abandon static-scene cues, but to **make them learned features** inside a supervised model. That is the difference between a brittle motion heuristic and a strong fixed-camera model.

If you do go balls-first, keep the scope narrow. Restrict detection to the court ROI; suppress detections in impossible regions; combine hard and soft tennis with **aggressive color augmentation**; and bootstrap from a teacher–student loop instead of labeling everything from scratch. In sports detection more broadly, semi-supervised teacher–student training improves results even when the labeled proportion is only **5–10%** of the available data, and high-confidence pseudo-labels outperform purely supervised teachers. That does not solve tennis-domain gap by itself, but it is exactly the right ingredient for using unlabeled match videos efficiently.

Synthetic data is a valid supporting tool here, but do not over-romanticize it. The strongest current synthetic-to-real paper in a closely related racket sport is in **table tennis**, where physically grounded synthetic training generalized to real monocular broadcast video with **92.0% spin classification** and **0.19% image-diagonal reprojection error**—without real training labels. That is encouraging, and it supports synthetic augmentation for white/low-contrast balls. But it is not a direct proof that a consumer-iPhone tennis ball detector will generalize from synthetic-only training. Treat synthetic data as a **cost reducer and edge-case augmenter**, not as the single magic bullet.

I do **not** recommend making TOTNet your first serious balls-first implementation. The research result is strong—especially under occlusion—but the engineering fit is poor. The model family uses 3D convolutions, optional optical flow, and an official **3-GPU** training recipe with NCCL; the best variant reports **8.66M params** and **12.19 FPS** average inference in the paper’s efficiency table. None of that is fatal for offline processing, but all of it is extra complexity for a product whose immediate KPI is only rally interval F1. TOTNet is attractive when ball-tracking itself is the product bottleneck. In your case, it is probably not.

## Data and Label Strategy

Your next labeling dollar should go to **more rally intervals**, not to frame-accurate ball points. Interval labels already supervise the actual task. If you downsample to 2–4 FPS and rasterize each frame as **play** or **non-play**, you obtain dense binary labels from the annotations you already maintain. That makes direct temporal segmentation unusually cost-effective for your project. The core problem right now is therefore not “wrong label type”; it is “far too little in-domain data.” Published models that succeed in racket sports nearly always train on more clips, more matches, or more frames than you currently have.

A realistic near-term target is to reach **roughly 30–60 total interval-labeled clips or matches** before you make a hard go/no-go judgment on the direct path. That is still small by research standards, but it is enough to expose whether the model is learning actual rally structure rather than memorizing clip identity. If labeling capacity is tight, bias collection toward diversity: both hard and soft tennis, bright and dim courts, different fences, one-ball and multi-ball practice settings, and clips with adjacent-court interference. The opportunity cost of not doing this is severe: each week spent re-tuning off-the-shelf broadcast trackers on eight clips is a week not spent reducing your true source of variance.

If the direct model reaches the high 0.7s but stalls below 0.85, the next label type to add is **sparse boundary supervision**, not full ball supervision. Timestamp-supervision literature is the key reason: sparse action timestamps can be competitive with full framewise supervision, and later work shows robustness even when timestamps are incomplete or loosely placed. In a user study, timestamp supervision still required only **0.03% of frames** labeled, though annotation time remained about **65.1%** of video duration because humans still had to watch and decide; the weaker SkipTag scheme cut that to **36.4%** of video duration. That is a strong argument for annotating a few extra semantic frames around difficult boundaries before jumping to thousands of ball clicks.

Only move to frame-level ball labels under two conditions: the direct path has already been given a fair data budget, and your roadmap genuinely benefits from ball-derived outputs beyond rally intervals. If you reach that point, start with a **small, high-value ball-label pool** on the hardest situations—not a blind full-dataset labeling campaign. The lesson from RacketVision is that sparse annotation plus dense pseudo-label generation is an efficient workflow; the lesson from semi-supervised sports detection and active-learning TAS is that human effort should target informative clips and frames, not uniform volume.

## Rejected Approaches Revisited

Your rejection of **motion-only / frame-difference / optical-flow heuristics** is justified. The hockey play-segmentation paper makes the same point in a different sport: optic flow alone is fallible because movement during breaks can still be substantial and movement during play can be limited, which is why they moved to deeper visual cues plus temporal modeling. What you should **not** conclude is that motion cues are useless. Recent racket-sport work shows that **explicit background representation** is valuable; the error was relying on handcrafted motion logic rather than letting a model learn how motion, context, and scene structure interact.

Your rejection of **off-the-shelf TrackNet V1 weights** is also justified. The original paper reports excellent numbers on its main evaluation, but once the authors widened the test condition with additional partially labeled videos and cross-validation, F1 dropped to **84.3%**. That is still respectable, but it is already evidence that this family is sensitive to distribution shift even within broadcast-like conditions. E2E-Spot separately notes large domain gaps between generic pretraining and fine-grained sports datasets, and soccer detection literature shows that sport- and venue-specific fine-tuning is often necessary. So your finding that a broadcast-trained ball model collapses on fixed-camera amateur footage is not a fluke; it is the expected outcome.

Your rejection of **audio-only, pose-only, and light rerankers as standalone solutions** is also correct. Tennis acoustics can detect atomic events very well—**92.39% F1** in one system—but point-boundary detection there still landed around **84/82** precision/recall for starts and **77/77** for ends, and that was in a specialized acoustic pipeline. Hockey work then shows audio becomes more useful only after unsupervised adaptation to game-specific domain shift. The correct conclusion is that these are **supporting modalities**, not good primary detectors for your app.

Your prediction that **WASB-SBDT off-the-shelf** would likely suffer from broadcast-domain mismatch is reasonable, but fully discarding its design ideas would be a mistake. WASB was built as a stronger multi-sport baseline with high-resolution features, position-aware training, and temporal consistency, and later RacketVision results reinforce that multi-sport training actually can help racket-sport generalization. So the right verdict is: **do not deploy broadcast-trained WASB weights as-is**, but do borrow its design logic if you pursue a retrained ball model.

## Commercial Signals

Public information about SwingVision strongly suggests that the commercial solution is a **multi-stage structured pipeline**, not a single monolithic end-to-end model. Consumer-facing materials say the app can run with a **single smartphone camera** and uses **on-device machine learning** without internet for video analysis. The ITF Player Analysis Technology approval report then discloses more of the stack: the system uses the rear camera of iPhones at **60 fps**, and “a series of machine learning models” outputs tracking information on the **ball, court, and players**. It also measures **3D ball locations** at racket hit, net contact, and bounce, plus shot type, spin type, and speed.

The patent evidence is even more revealing. SwingVision’s granted patent, assigned to Mangolytics / SwingVision, describes a mobile-device system that learns **3D properties** from 2D images by combining pixels depicting the object with pixels depicting **regulation court topology**. It further describes training on **2D images paired with 3D sensor measurements** from prior instrumented events. In plain language, the likely commercial recipe is: calibrate the court, track ball/players/court, infer 3D trajectory or events from those structured cues, and train with richer ground truth than an ordinary phone user could label manually.

There is an important caveat for your product decisions: the ITF tournament-approved configuration is **not identical** to your “fully offline, one-device” constraint. The PAT report explicitly says data is transferred to a backend server via WiFi or cellular and allows an optional second smartphone and tablet in the approved setup. So while SwingVision’s consumer product publicly markets on-device analysis, the tournament-approved commercial architecture uses a broader system envelope than the one you can allow. That means “copy SwingVision” is not a real option. You need to copy the **useful parts of the architecture**, not the whole system boundary.

The actionable implication is harsh but useful: **SwingVision’s balls-first stack makes economic sense because they monetize line calling, shot analytics, and officiating**. You do not. For a product whose near-term KPI is just rally interval draft quality, their likely architecture is overbuilt. The lesson to import is not “build 3D ball reconstruction first”; it is “use court structure and multiple specialized heads.” For you, the first specialized heads should be **rally-state** and **boundary** heads, not a full line-calling-grade ball stack.

## Open Questions and Source Notes

Two things remain genuinely unresolved in the public literature. First, there is **very little directly published on fixed-camera amateur tennis rally-interval Event F1**, especially for **soft tennis** with a white ball. Most of the strongest cited work measures mAP, per-frame tracking accuracy, RMSE, or activity-segmentation accuracy in other sports or in broadcast tennis. Second, I did **not** find official documentation in the TrackNetV4 or TOTNet repos that shows a polished **Core ML / TFLite export path**; both repos expose training and evaluation code, but not an end-to-end mobile deployment recipe.

The most load-bearing sources behind the recommendation are these. For the **balls-free direct path**, the key papers are **E2E-Spot** for compact precise event spotting, **Temporal Action Segmentation from Timestamp Supervision**, and the later robust timestamp-supervision / active-learning TAS papers. For the **balls-first conditional path**, the key evidence is **TrackNetV4**, **TOTNet**, **WASB-SBDT**, **RacketVision**, and the semi-supervised soccer detection paper. For the **commercial decomposition**, the decisive sources are the **ITF PAT approval report** and **SwingVision’s granted patent**. For edge deployment constraints, the decisive sources are **Apple’s Core ML / coremltools docs** and **TensorFlow Lite conversion / quantization docs**.
